Privacy-preserving quantum federated learning

A three-month internship spent asking one question: if a federated learning update is 32 real numbers, and 5 qubits can hold exactly 32 amplitudes, can you send the quantum object instead and save bandwidth? Once every cost is counted honestly, no. Showing exactly where and why is the actual contribution.

02 — The easy half

32 numbers onto 5 qubits

A quantum state on n qubits holds 2n amplitudes at once, and 25 is exactly 32. So the update gets split into two parts: a length — one number, sent as an ordinary classical float — and a direction, the 32 values rescaled so their own length is exactly 1, because a quantum state is required to have unit length.

Square those 32 direction values and they sum to exactly 1. That's a probability distribution over the 32 possible measurement outcomes of 5 qubits, which is precisely what the qubits can represent. Loading it on is cheap and it works.

Getting the numbers onto the qubits was never the problem. Getting them back off is.

03 — The sign problem

Measurement throws away the minus signs

Measuring a quantum state returns a squared amplitude — a probability. Probabilities are never negative, so the sign of every one of those 32 numbers vanishes the moment you look. Three real strategies for dealing with that, compared on identical data and identical measurements:

Strategy
How it handles sign
What it costs
Classical side-channel
Sign is sent separately as plain bits, exactly. Only magnitude is measured, so sign is never wrong — noise only ever affects size.
5 qubits + 64 bits
Interference readout
Sign is recovered from the measurement itself using an ancilla qubit. Converges to the right answer, but needs roughly twice the shots — splitting each measurement across two outcomes and subtracting is a less efficient use of every shot.
6 qubits + 32 bits
Signs discarded
No sign handling at all; every value is guessed positive. Cheapest, and it never improves no matter how much you spend — the error doesn't respond to effort.
5 qubits + 32 bits

Discarding sign is worse than it sounds. In a typical harvested update, the parameters with negative true values held 69% of the total energy — so guessing positive is wrong most often on exactly the components that matter most.

04 — The core math

Why the gap widens with accuracy

Classical compression and quantum measurement improve at fundamentally different rates, and this is the whole result in one line. Each extra classical bit roughly halves the error. Each extra quantum shot buys only a square root's worth.

The classical curve is fitted to this project's own quant2 / quant4 / quant8 measurements: error ≈ 2.4 × 2−1.14×bits. The quantum curve isn't fitted at all — it's the closed form derived from sampling statistics, and it matched real measurements to within 4%: relative error ≈ 2.83 / √shots.

05 — Honest accounting

Every scheme has two prices

There's the optimistic price — count a single copy of the quantum state, 5 or 6 qubits, done — and the honest price, which counts every shot actually needed to read it back. Every shot is a fresh copy that has to be sent. For classical schemes the two numbers are identical. For quantum they are not close.

Sending the raw 32 numbers plainly costs 1,024 bits. That's the line worth measuring against: anything above it costs more than not compressing at all. Under honest accounting, the quantum schemes cross that line quickly.

The naive bars don't move when you spend more. The honest ones do — which is the entire point.

06 — Future work, tested

Does combining before measuring help?

The obvious proposed fix: combine the three nodes' quantum states before anyone measures, so the server reads out one shared average instead of paying separately for each node. Whether it helps turns out to depend entirely on how alike the three updates already are.

If the three point in unrelated directions, combining first wins by close to its theoretical ceiling. If they already point the same way, there's nothing to gain and the two approaches tie. This project's own harvested updates measured a mean pairwise alignment of about 0.72 — well toward the aligned end — so the real, measured benefit came out modest: roughly 20% fewer shots for equal accuracy.

What this doesn't account for

The 20% figure prices the read-out saving only. It does not price the cost of physically combining three separate quantum states before measurement, which is a real and substantial obstacle on its own.

The alignment sweep in the demo uses synthetic vectors built so alignment can move smoothly; the marker shows where the real data actually sits. The interference mode shown is a calibrated approximation matched to this project's measured error ratio, not a literal reproduction of the circuit's exact probabilities.

08 — Why it mattered to me

The value of a clean negative

I came to this from an applied AI background, and the instinct that degree gives you is to make something work. A second master's on the cognitive science track has been about the opposite instinct: sit with a problem on a fundamental mathematical basis until you understand what it can and can't do, before you commit to it.

This project needed both. The theory tells you where the square-root wall is. The applied side insists that a theory is only as good as its viability inside the system it has to live in — and that publishing "we tried this and here is precisely how it fails, with the numbers" is more useful to the next person than a result nudged into looking positive.

That's also why the companion recomputes everything live instead of shipping figures. If the finding is real, it should survive being poked at.
