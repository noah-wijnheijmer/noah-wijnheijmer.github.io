The Core Idea

32 Numbers, 5 Qubits

An update is 32 real numbers. A quantum state on n qubits can hold 2n amplitudes at once, and 25 is exactly 32. So instead of sending 32 separate numbers, the idea is to load them onto 5 qubits as one object. Here's what that loading step actually does to a real update.

Where this fits

The 32 raw numbers are split into a length (one number, sent as a plain classical float) and a direction (32 numbers, always rescaled so their own length is exactly 1). Only the direction goes onto qubits, because a quantum state is required to have unit length. Square each direction value and you get 32 probabilities that sum to exactly 1, which is what 5 qubits can represent: one probability per possible outcome, 25 outcomes in total.

Loading these 32 numbers onto 5 qubits is the easy, cheap half of the story. The Sign Recovery and Bits vs Shots tabs cover the hard half: reading them back out.

Why the interference method sits in between

The classical side-channel never gets sign wrong. It's told the sign directly, exactly, so only magnitude carries any noise. Signs discarded never gets sign right in any way that responds to effort, since it never attempts to measure sign at all. Interference sits between them on purpose: it recovers sign from the measurement itself using an extra ancilla qubit, so it converges toward a perfect answer just like the classical side-channel does, but it needs roughly twice the shots to get there, because splitting the measurement across two outcomes and subtracting them is a less efficient use of each shot.

In the vector you're looking at, parameters with a negative true value hold 69% of the total energy. That's why signs-discarded settles near −0.386 instead of 0: always guessing positive is wrong most often on exactly the components that matter most.

The interference mode shown here is a calibrated approximation matched to this project's measured error ratio (about 2× the classical side-channel's shots for equal fidelity), not a literal reproduction of the underlying circuit's exact probabilities.

The Core Math

Why the Gap Widens With Accuracy

Classical compression and quantum measurement improve at fundamentally different rates. Each extra classical bit roughly halves the error. Each extra quantum shot buys only a square root's worth. Pick a target accuracy below and see what each approach actually costs to reach it.

Where these numbers come from

The classical curve is fitted to this project's own quant2 / quant4 / quant8 measurements: roughly, error ≈ 2.4 × 2−1.14×bits. The quantum curve is not fitted, it's the closed form derived from sampling statistics and confirmed against real measurements to within 4%: relative error ≈ 2.83 / √shots.

Future Work, Tested Live

Does Combining Before Measuring Actually Help?

One proposed fix: combine the three nodes' quantum states before anyone measures, so the server reads out one shared average instead of paying to read each node separately. Whether that helps turns out to depend entirely on how alike the three updates already are.

What the slider is doing

At the far right, all three nodes point in essentially the same direction, and the two approaches tie: there's nothing to gain from combining first. At the far left, the three directions are unrelated, and combining first wins by close to its theoretical ceiling, bounded by the number of nodes. This project's own harvested updates measured a mean pairwise alignment of about 0.72, marked above, which is why the real, measured benefit came out modest (about 20% fewer shots for equal accuracy) rather than dramatic.

These are synthetic vectors built to let alignment move smoothly, not the project's real updates, though the marker shows where real data actually sits. This also doesn't price the cost of physically combining three separate quantum states before measurement, which is a real, unpriced obstacle on its own.
